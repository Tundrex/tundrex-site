CFU Count Is a Marketing Myth: What Probiotic Viability Actually Means

July 2026 · 8 min read · Tundrex Editorial Team

Walk into any pharmacy or health food store and you'll see probiotic labels competing on one number above all others: CFU count. 10 billion. 50 billion. 100 billion. The implicit message is clear — bigger is better, and more organisms mean more benefit.

But this CFU arms race is, in large part, a marketing illusion. The number of Colony Forming Units printed on a label tells you almost nothing about how many live organisms actually reach your intestines where they're needed. And it tells you nothing about whether those organisms can do anything useful once they arrive.

Understanding what probiotic viability really means — and why spore-based strains operate under an entirely different set of rules — is essential if you want to make an informed decision about your gut health protocol.

What Is a CFU, and Why Does It Fall Short as a Measure of Probiotic Quality?

CFU stands for Colony Forming Unit — a measure of the number of viable (capable of reproducing) microorganisms in a sample, determined by culturing the sample in a lab and counting the colonies that form. It's a standard microbiological method, and it's not inherently dishonest.

The problem is when that count is taken, and what it doesn't account for. Most CFU counts on probiotic labels represent the organism count at the time of manufacture, often accompanied by a note that the count is guaranteed "at time of production" rather than at expiration. By the time that capsule reaches your mouth, the living population inside may be substantially lower — sometimes dramatically so.

From the moment of production, conventional probiotic strains like Lactobacillus acidophilus and Bifidobacterium longum face a continuous attrition problem:

  • Temperature sensitivity: Many non-spore-forming strains require refrigeration to maintain viability. Time on a warehouse shelf, in a delivery truck, or in your pantry at room temperature chips away at the living population continuously.
  • Oxygen exposure: Several key Lactobacillus strains are microaerophilic — they prefer low-oxygen environments. Capsule manufacturing and storage inevitably expose them to ambient air, accelerating die-off.
  • Moisture and humidity: Even small fluctuations in humidity during packaging or storage can trigger premature germination or death in fragile strains.
  • Time: Biological organisms don't stay alive indefinitely in a capsule. A product with a two-year shelf life may have lost a significant fraction of its labeled CFUs by month six under imperfect storage conditions — conditions that are, in practice, the norm rather than the exception.

None of these losses are reflected on the label. You may be buying 50 billion CFUs and consuming something closer to 5 billion — or fewer.

The Stomach Acid Gauntlet: Where Most CFUs Go to Die

Even if the organisms in your capsule survive from production to swallowing, they then face the most hostile environment in the human body: your stomach. During active digestion, gastric pH can drop to between 1.5 and 3.5 — strongly acidic conditions that are lethal to the majority of non-spore-forming bacteria.

Research published in the Journal of Applied Microbiology and other peer-reviewed sources has consistently documented significant viability losses for Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium strains exposed to simulated gastric acid. In some studies, survival rates through simulated GI transit have been documented at less than 30% — and in poorly optimized formulations, closer to 1–5%.

The math compounds quickly. If a product labeled at 50 billion CFUs has already lost 60% of its organisms to storage degradation, you're swallowing roughly 20 billion. If 70% of those fail to survive gastric transit, you're delivering around 6 billion viable organisms to the small intestine — a far cry from the headline number, and potentially below the therapeutic threshold for the health benefit you're seeking.

This is not a fringe critique. The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) and researchers in gut microbiology have raised similar concerns about the disconnect between labeled CFU counts and actual delivered viability for many widely sold probiotic formulations.

The Viability Gap

Between manufacturing die-off, shelf degradation, and stomach acid exposure, a significant fraction of the CFUs on a conventional probiotic label never reach your intestines alive. The number on the label is, at best, a starting estimate — not a delivery guarantee.

Why Enteric Coating Isn't the Whole Answer

The supplement industry has recognized this problem for years and responded with enteric-coated capsules — formulations with a coating designed to resist stomach acid and dissolve only in the more alkaline environment of the small intestine. This is a genuine improvement over uncoated capsules, and enteric coating does improve GI transit survival for many strains.

But it's not a complete solution. Enteric coatings can be inconsistent in their dissolution timing — dissolving too early in the stomach, or too late to allow optimal colonization in the small intestine. The coating itself adds manufacturing complexity and cost. And it does nothing to address the temperature sensitivity and oxygen vulnerability that degrade organisms before the capsule is even consumed.

More fundamentally, enteric coating is an engineering workaround for a biological limitation. It's an attempt to protect organisms that were not naturally designed to survive outside the human body by wrapping them in a synthetic shell. There's nothing wrong with that approach per se — but it's worth asking whether there's a better starting point.

Spore-Based Probiotics: Built-In Survival, No Engineering Required

Spore-forming bacteria like Bacillus subtilis and Bacillus coagulans don't need enteric coating, refrigeration, or elaborate protective chemistry. They have evolved their own survival mechanism over hundreds of millions of years: the endospore.

When environmental conditions become inhospitable — whether extreme heat, desiccation, UV radiation, or acid — spore-forming bacteria form a dormant endospore. This structure encases the organism's genetic material within a series of interlocking protein layers reinforced by calcium dipicolinate and other compounds that create a physical and chemical shield of remarkable resilience.

Documented endospore survival data is striking:

  • Endospores of Bacillus subtilis have been shown to survive exposure to temperatures above 100°C
  • They withstand stomach acid at pH as low as 1.0 — conditions that would destroy virtually all Lactobacillus strains within minutes
  • They remain viable at room temperature for years, with no refrigeration required
  • They survive UV irradiation that would be lethal to non-spore-forming organisms

This means the CFU equation looks radically different for spore-based probiotics. The organism count at manufacture is, in practice, very close to the organism count at consumption — and the proportion of those organisms that survive gastric transit and arrive viable in the small intestine is dramatically higher than for conventional strains.

CFU vs. Spore Viability: The Right Way to Compare

When comparing a spore-based probiotic to a conventional Lactobacillus product, a raw CFU-to-CFU comparison is meaningless. A conventional probiotic with 50 billion CFUs may deliver fewer live organisms to the intestines than a spore-based product with 5 billion — because the attrition rates across the supply chain and GI tract are so fundamentally different.

A more useful comparison framework considers:

  • Stability at room temperature — does the organism survive from factory to gut without refrigeration?
  • Gastric acid survival rate — what proportion of organisms in the capsule actually survive transit to the small intestine?
  • Colonization and germination — do the surviving organisms establish themselves in the intestinal environment and become metabolically active?
  • Ecological behavior — what does the organism actually do once it's active? Does it support beneficial species, produce useful metabolites, or simply pass through?

On each of these dimensions, spore-based strains like Bacillus subtilis compare favorably to conventional alternatives — not because of marketing claims, but because of basic microbiology.

A Better Question Than "How Many CFUs?"

Instead of asking how many Colony Forming Units are listed on the label, ask: How many of those organisms will reach my intestines alive? For spore-based probiotics, the answer is close to 100%. For most conventional strains, it is considerably less.

What Happens After Germination: Why Spore Behavior Matters

Survival through the GI tract is only half the story. What an organism does once it arrives is equally important — and this is where Bacillus subtilis again differentiates itself from conventional probiotic strains.

Once the endospore encounters the pH-neutral environment of the small intestine, it germinates: the protective coat is shed, the organism becomes metabolically active, and it begins interacting with the gut environment. Research has documented several clinically significant behaviors in germinated Bacillus subtilis:

  • Bacteriocin production: Bacillus subtilis produces natural antimicrobial peptides (bacteriocins) that selectively suppress pathogenic and opportunistic bacteria — including certain strains of E. coli, Salmonella, and Clostridium — without disturbing beneficial species.
  • Support for commensal species: Published microbiome studies have shown that Bacillus subtilis supplementation is associated with increased populations of endogenous Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium — suggesting an ecosystem-organizing role rather than simple competition for space.
  • Short-chain fatty acid production: Germinated Bacillus subtilis produces butyrate and other SCFAs, which are essential fuel for enterocytes (intestinal lining cells), support tight junction integrity, and influence immune signaling through the gut-associated lymphoid tissue.
  • Immune modulation: Research has documented that Bacillus subtilis and its metabolites interact with toll-like receptors (TLRs) and other pattern recognition receptors in gut-associated immune tissue, helping calibrate immune responses toward tolerance and away from chronic inflammatory activation.

This profile of downstream activity explains why Dr. Galland has described Bacillus subtilis as an "Alexander organism" — meaning it doesn't passively occupy a microbial niche but actively helps reorganize and restore balance within the broader microbial community. The CFU number on the label doesn't capture any of this. Spore viability and post-germination behavior are the metrics that actually matter.

What This Means for Choosing a Probiotic

None of this means that conventional Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium probiotics are without value. There is genuine clinical evidence for specific strains in specific contexts — particularly certain Lactobacillus rhamnosus and Bifidobacterium infantis strains for gastrointestinal conditions. The evidence base is real, even if the marketing that surrounds it is often inflated.

What it does mean is that CFU count alone is a deeply inadequate criterion for choosing a probiotic. If you've tried high-CFU conventional products without noticeable results, the viability gap is the most likely explanation. The organisms on the label may not have been making it to the destination in meaningful numbers.
